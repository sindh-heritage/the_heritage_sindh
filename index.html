02 — A PLACE BEFORE OUR TIMETHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

A city in Sindh.
A window into the past.

Our journey begins near Larkana, in today’s Sindh, Pakistan. Here lie the remains of Muan Jo Daro, one of the great cities of the Indus Civilization.

More than 4,000 years ago, people built homes, made useful objects, and organized life together here. Let’s discover what they left behind.

Explore the UNESCO record ↗

Why do we use two names?＋

Muan Jo Daro is the name we use for this journey. You will also see Mohenjo-daro or Moenjodaro in books, museums, and UNESCO records. They refer to the same archaeological site.

04 — THE CITY’S EVERYDAY INGENUITYTHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

Follow a drop.
Discover a city.

Imagine fetching water from a brick-lined well. Nearby, people wash and carry out their daily work.

Wells and drains are among the clues that show how carefully this city managed water. Its builders thought about everyday needs as well as buildings.

Was this like turning on a modern tap?＋

The illustration connects ideas, not actual pipes. Wells supplied water that people could collect; drainage helped carry used water away. We should not imagine a modern pressurized water supply.

05 — LOOK CLOSER AT ONE GREAT DISCOVERYTHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

A bath built
to hold a mystery.

At the Great Bath, steps lead down into a large brick-lined tank. Carefully fitted bricks and bitumen—a natural tar—helped keep water from seeping through.

The structure survives. A certain explanation of its purpose does not. Archaeologists often discuss a possible ceremonial use, but that remains an interpretation.

See the archaeological photographs ↗

Try it: what do the steps tell us?＋

They show that people could enter and leave the tank. They do not, by themselves, prove exactly which activities took place there. Good archaeology separates observations from interpretations.

06 — THE PEOPLE BEHIND THE BRICKSTHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

Homes had people.
Objects have stories.

Think beyond the walls: making a pot, shaping a bead, or weighing something for exchange. Objects help us imagine the work and choices of people whose names we do not know.

Pottery, ornaments, toys, and weights from Indus sites offer clues about daily life and skilled craft. The illustrations here represent types of objects, not copies of individual finds.

What might a weight tell us?＋

Weights recovered at Mohenjo-daro suggest careful measurement in exchanges. They help archaeologists study how trade was organized.

What can’t an object tell us on its own?＋

A pot can show material, shape, and craft technique. It usually cannot tell us its owner’s name, what they felt, or every way they used it.

07 — WHAT THE EARTH KEPTTHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

A city uncovered.
A story unfinished.

Archaeological excavation began at Mohenjo-daro in 1922. Walls and objects brought an ancient city into a new field of study.

Much remains uncertain. The Indus inscriptions have no generally accepted decipherment. Scholars also study several factors in the decline and transformation of Indus urban life; a single dramatic ending would oversimplify that history.

Can we read the Indus signs?＋

Not with an agreed decipherment. We can study where the signs appear and how they are arranged, but should not present an invented translation as fact.

Did everyone disappear overnight?＋

There is no basis for telling this as a sudden disappearance of an entire people. Changes in cities and settlement patterns were complex. Their timing and causes are still studied.

08 — THE NEXT CHAPTER BELONGS TO USTHE MUAN JO DARO STORY

Ancient bricks.
A future in our hands.

Muan Jo Daro joined the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1980. Its exposed remains need ongoing care: salts, rain, heat, and flooding can damage ancient brickwork.

Respect barriers, leave objects where they are, and follow the site’s visiting rules. Sharing accurate stories also helps protect its meaning.

Learn about conservation work ↗

What should we remember about this city?＋

Its planned buildings, attention to water, and surviving objects reveal an extraordinary ancient community. Its unanswered questions remind us to stay curious and careful with evidence.

11 — LISTEN TO THE SOUL✧

POETRY, WISDOM & MUSIC

Some stories
aren’t written.
They are sung.

For generations, Sindh’s poets and musicians have given voice to love, courage, humanity, and devotion.

Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai’s poetry lives on in Shah jo Risalo and the musical tradition of Shah jo Raag. Instruments such as the tamburo and yaktaro carry the spirit of folk expression.

About this journey.

This educational exhibition puts Muan Jo Daro at the heart of Sindh’s story. Eight opening chapters follow the city from its setting and everyday ingenuity to discovery and preservation. Five closing chapters introduce the river and Sindh’s living cultural traditions. They are not presented as proof of an unchanged culture across millennia.

All scenes are original, stylized illustrations. The ancient city and architectural panorama are artistic interpretations, not exact archaeological reconstructions. The blue drainage line is illustrative, and the Ajrak printing sequence simplifies a much longer craft process. The optional audio is an original synthesized ambient composition, not a recording of Sindhi folk music.
